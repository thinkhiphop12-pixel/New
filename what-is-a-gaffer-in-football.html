What Is a Gaffer in Football?

One of the most-used words in an English dressing room, and one of the most confusing if you did not grow up around it.

Updated 7 August 2026 · BALLKNW

Short answer

In British and Irish football, the gaffer is the manager — the person who picks the team, sets the tactics and runs the first-team squad. It is informal but respectful, roughly equivalent to calling someone the boss. It refers to one person, not the coaching staff as a group.

What the word actually means

If you hear a player say "the gaffer wants us pressing higher," they are talking about the manager. Not the assistant, not the coaching team, not the owner — the individual whose name goes next to the club's in the league table and who carries the blame when results turn.

It is squarely informal. A club will never announce that it has "appointed a new gaffer"; the press release says manager or head coach. But in a post-match interview, a dressing room, a pub, or a phone-in, gaffer is completely standard and carries no disrespect. If anything it carries warmth — it is the word you use about a manager you have played under.

"The gaffer pulled me at half-time and told me to stay wider." — the kind of sentence that makes the usage obvious in context.

Where "gaffer" comes from

The word is much older than football, and its exact origin is not settled.

Gaffer appears in English from around the 16th century as a form of address for an older man — the male counterpart to gammer. The two derivations most often proposed are from godfather or from grandfather, in each case worn down by everyday speech. Dictionaries generally present these as probable rather than proven, so treat anyone stating one of them as definite fact with mild suspicion.

From "respected older man," the sense drifted to "the man in charge of the work" — a foreman, an overseer, the person running a crew. That is the meaning football inherited. It is also the meaning the film industry inherited independently, which is why the chief lighting technician on a film set is also called a gaffer. The two usages are cousins, not borrowings from one another.

Why football picked it up

English professional football grew up in industrial towns, among players who had worked or whose families worked in trades where the foreman was the gaffer. Applying the same word to the man running the team was not a leap — it was the obvious word to hand. The usage stuck and outlived the industries it came from.

Who says it — and who doesn't

Usage is strongly regional.

England, Scotland, Wales and Ireland: completely normal. Players, ex-players on punditry duty, commentators and supporters all use it freely.

United States: rare. American soccer uses coach as the default and head coach as the formal title. If an American hears "gaffer" it is usually from a British commentator on a Premier League broadcast — or, far more likely in everyday life, it means the lighting chief on a film set. That ambiguity is worth knowing: search for "gaffer" in the US and you will get film crews and gaffer tape long before you get football.

Elsewhere: largely absent, though it travels with English-language football coverage and turns up among fans of English clubs worldwide.

Gaffer vs manager vs coach

These are not perfect synonyms, and the distinction is genuinely meaningful in the English game.

TermRegisterWhat it refers to
GafferInformalThe manager, as spoken about by players, staff and fans
ManagerFormalTraditionally broad authority — selection, tactics, and historically recruitment and transfers too
Head coachFormalNarrower remit focused on coaching and matchday, with recruitment sitting elsewhere (a sporting director, for instance)
CoachFormalA specialist working under the manager — goalkeeping, set pieces, fitness

The manager/head-coach split is a live distinction in English football, as more clubs adopt the continental model where a sporting director handles signings. Gaffer tends to get applied to whoever is visibly in charge of the team regardless of which title the club has given them.

Other names for the manager

Boss — the closest equivalent in England, used almost interchangeably with gaffer.

Mister — standard in Spain, Portugal and Italy, borrowed from the English coaches who helped establish the professional game in those countries and never given back.

Their first name — increasingly common, and usually a signal of a flatter, more informal dressing room.

Common questions

What is a gaffer in football?

The manager or head coach of a football club. It is an informal British and Irish term used by players, staff, pundits and supporters — roughly equivalent to calling someone the boss. It refers to one specific person: the individual in charge of team selection, tactics and the first-team squad.

Why is a football manager called the gaffer?

The word predates football. In British English a gaffer was a foreman or the man in charge of a work crew, and the term carried into industries with a clear chain of command — including football and film. Players calling the manager gaffer is the same instinct as a crew calling their foreman boss: informal, but a mark of authority.

What is the origin of the word gaffer?

Not settled. The word appears in English from around the 16th century as a form of address for an older man, and the most commonly cited theories derive it from either godfather or grandfather, worn down through everyday speech. From there it broadened to mean a foreman. Dictionaries generally present these derivations as probable rather than certain.

Do Americans say gaffer in soccer?

Rarely. American soccer uses coach as standard and head coach as the formal title. Gaffer is heard in the US mostly from British and Irish commentators, or from fans who follow the Premier League. In American English the word far more often means the chief lighting technician on a film set.

Is a gaffer the same as a coach?

Not exactly. In the English game the manager traditionally holds broader authority than a coach — selection, tactics, and historically transfers — while coaches work under them on specific areas such as goalkeeping or set pieces. Gaffer refers to the person at the top of that structure.

Can a woman be called the gaffer?

Yes. The word is not grammatically gendered in modern usage and is applied to managers in the women's game as well as the men's, though boss and manager are heard at least as often.

What does gaffer mean outside football?

In film and television, the gaffer is the chief lighting technician — the head of the electrical department on set. In older or regional British usage it can still simply mean a foreman, or an elderly man. Gaffer tape takes its name from the film-set sense.
